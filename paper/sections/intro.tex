\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Graph-based approximate nearest neighbor search (ANNS) is a production serving primitive: a query walks a proximity graph and scores neighbors until a beam budget is spent.
At billions of vectors the graph plus full-precision embeddings no longer fit in host DRAM, so the corpus must live behind another tier.
CXL makes that tier addressable with \texttt{load}/\texttt{store}.
When the whole corpus fits in CXL-DRAM, prior systems already offer a usable service---CXL-ANNS and Cosmos~\cite{cxlanns,cxlanns-tocs,cosmos} never stall on flash.
This paper is about the next device.
Flash-backed CXL memory (CXL-SSD) exposes NAND capacity and only a small DRAM-fast window on a CXL.mem address space~\cite{skybyte,fromblocktobyte,cmm-h,cxl30}.
A unified VA does not make graph search usable: a window miss costs tens of microseconds.
To our knowledge, \sys{} is the first \emph{graph-ANNS serving runtime} that operates directly over an SSD-backed CXL address space and makes \emph{scoring} usable---the host computes full-precision distances from bytes that already sit in that window.
We do not claim the first CXL-DRAM ANNS, the first CXL-SSD attached to a vector database, or a product CXL.mem SSD.

Two published lines each solve one side of the capacity--latency tension and tax the other (Figure~\ref{fig:intro-position}).
DiskANN~\cite{diskann,freshdiskann} and pipelined descendants such as PipeANN~\cite{pipeann} are excellent \emph{block-SSD} ANNS: PQ navigation, sector layout, and asynchronous I/O hide NAND behind a per-host cache.
Their serving unit remains CPU $+$ DRAM working set $+$ a local SSD index.
Adding $R$ query workers typically replicates provisioned flash or shards the graph.
They hide \emph{block I/O}, not a faulting CXL address space.
CXL-ANNS and Cosmos put the graph and embeddings in a CXL-DRAM pool.
Search never sees flash, and a pooled HDM can be one copy---but the cold tail $D-H$ is priced at DRAM~\cite{bauhaus,secondtier}.
We build a third service because neither line is the right contract for CXL-SSD: the corpus should stay flash-priced, the hot path should stay memory-semantic, and the runtime must hide NAND from the scoring instruction.
Multi-host one-copy under Shared FAM is a deployment path we do not evaluate (\S\ref{sec:background}).

Making that service real is hard because the device is not slightly slow DRAM and not a DiskANN SSD.
Three hardware facts break conventional designs~(\S\ref{sec:motivation}).
\emph{(C1)~The miss cliff.}
A window hit is a DRAM-class copy; a miss is a NAND fill ($\sim$40--80\,$\mu$s, about $140\times$).
Graph walk multiplies the tax by hops $\times$ degree, so treating CXL-SSD as mmap'd memory makes search miss-bound.
\emph{(C2)~Prefetch must be wise.}
Neighbor IDs are not sequential.
OS-style page admission and synchronous 2-hop graph prefetch bring vectors the walk will not score, pollute the small window, and can drop QPS by $3$--$5\times$.
Wrong pages cost more than a missed page.
\emph{(C3)~One query cannot fill the device.}
Best-first search cannot issue hop $i{+}1$'s vector pages until hop $i$ scores.
A blocking load/store miss collapses queue depth to $\approx 1$; a single walk leaves the NAND pipeline idle even when bandwidth remains.
Continuous issue across queries is what holds depth; it does not, by itself, make one walk as fast as an in-window oracle.

\sys{} is a runtime for those three constraints, not a new graph index.
A placement premise comes first: the adjacency list and any PQ codebook live in host DRAM so the prefetcher can read neighbor IDs without a NAND miss; full-precision vectors stay on CXL-SSD.
The first mechanism is a \emph{wise prefetcher}.
It issues asynchronous fills only for pages the current frontier is about to expand, packs co-scored neighbors into the same pages (expand-bundle / page-bin), and admits them under a byte budget.
It never synchronous-walks a 2-hop neighborhood and never scores unused siblings to inflate utilization.
The second mechanism is \emph{continuous batching}.
Fills are issued at fetch granularity across in-flight queries so that when one walk is stuck on pointer-chasing, another query keeps the device busy.
Prefetch and batching may touch CXL-SSD; the scoring thread may not.
We call that split \emph{score hide}.
The stronger contract---\emph{query hide}, no wait for a fill on the timed path---is the oracle, not a result we claim.
On true-cold Text2Image-10M (recall@10 $\ge 0.92$, $L{=}400$), score hide holds (\texttt{from\_win}${=}100\%$) at $50.25$~QPS versus $1.64$~QPS demand, but remains $0.59\times$ an in-window oracle ($85.8$~QPS) because hop $i{+}1$ cannot issue until hop $i$ scores.

This paper makes four contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A third serving point, scoped.}
    We place graph ANNS on flash-backed CXL memory and separate the \emph{opportunity} (flash-priced $D$, a small window) from the \emph{system} (hide NAND from scoring).
    We do not claim the first CXL-DRAM ANNS or an evaluated Shared-FAM deployment~(\S\ref{sec:background}).
  \item \textbf{Why naive CXL-SSD ANNS fails.}
    We measure the miss cliff, unwise page/prefetch admission, and single-query queue collapse, and we turn those negatives into design constraints~(\S\ref{sec:motivation}).
  \item \textbf{A wise prefetcher plus continuous batching.}
    Placement keeps neighbor IDs off the window; the prefetcher fills only soon-to-expand pages; batching holds device depth across walks.
    Score hide reaches $100\%$ window hits on the scoring path; query hide remains $0.59\times$ the in-window oracle~(\S\ref{sec:design}, \S\ref{sec:impl}).
  \item \textbf{An iso-recall evaluation of hide, not just QPS.}
    We report scores-from-window, critical-path wait, and QPS from demand ($1.64$) through \sys{} ($50.25$) to the oracle ($85.8$).
    Same-machine PipeANN is a named residual (oneshot full-precision versus PQ navigation), not a hidden bar~(\S\ref{sec:eval}).
\end{enumerate}
